01

Remember

9 minutes

Last week, the valley stayed dark—but the Shepherd stayed too. This week asks how we remember Him when the next hard thing comes.

Where we are

Psalms 77 and 78 sit next to each other for a reason. Psalm 77 is one person in the middle of a terrible night, trying to remember whether God is still there. Psalm 78 is the answer a whole people give their children: tell the old rescue stories again.

The Israelites repeated the Red Sea, the manna, and the wilderness because forgetfulness was not just losing a fact. When they forgot what God had done, they stopped trusting what He could do next.

What did He do?

A remembered rescue becomes courage for the next problem.

Tap a memory.
Ask after all six
  • Which story would you want to remember on a scary day?
  • What does that story prove about God?
  • Why does remembering an old rescue help with a brand-new problem?

Land it: We remember God’s works so we can set our hope in Him—not so we can win a Bible trivia game.

02

The turn

6 minutes

Psalm 77 begins with someone awake all night, too upset to be comforted. Scripture keeps the hard questions too.

Meet Asaph

The writer is Asaph, a temple musician. His job was literally to help other people worship, and even he has a night when prayer feels unanswered. He remembers earlier songs and happier days, but that almost makes the present hurt more.

Then he asks the questions frightened people still ask: Has God stopped loving me? Has mercy run out? Did He forget His promise? The psalm does not scold him for asking.

“Thou holdest mine eyes waking: I am so troubled that I cannot speak.”Psalm 77:4
“Hath God forgotten to be gracious? hath he in anger shut up his tender mercies?”Psalm 77:9
Before“Has God forgotten me?”
→
The hinge“I will remember.”

Nothing outside changes at verse 11. Asaph is still awake; the trouble is still real. What changes is where he points his mind. He stops making predictions from one terrible night and starts gathering evidence from God’s whole history.

“I will meditate also of all thy work, and talk of thy doings.”Psalm 77:12
“Thy way is in the sea … and thy footsteps are not known.”Psalm 77:19
Ask
  • What changed: Asaph’s problem, or what he was looking at?
  • Why might God’s path be real even when His footprints are invisible?

Land it: Faith does not require pretending the night is easy. It remembers that this night is not the whole story.

03

Return

9 minutes

David made a choice he could not undo or excuse. He did not ask God to pretend it was fine. He asked God to change him.

What Psalm 51 is

This is not David feeling bad because he got caught. He finally sees the damage clearly and comes to God without a defense. Notice his verbs: wash me, cleanse me, create in me, renew me, restore me. David can confess and turn, but he knows he cannot manufacture a new heart by himself.

That is why repentance is hopeful. Its center is not how dirty we are; its center is what Jesus Christ can clean, heal, restore, and remake.

“Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin.”Psalm 51:2
“Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean: wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow.”Psalm 51:7
“Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me.”Psalm 51:10
Open the heart

Tap each closed place

Repentance is how Jesus helps us come back.

A clean heart is a heart that let Jesus Christ in.

🪨

When my heart is overwhelmed, lead me to the rock that is higher than I. — Psalm 61:2

“Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation.”Psalm 51:12
“A broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.”Psalm 51:17

Have everyone make two tight fists: hiding, defending, holding on. Then open both hands. A contrite heart is not a destroyed heart. It is an honest, open one.

Ask
  • Which word sounds most hopeful: wash, create, renew, or restore?
  • What can we do after a wrong choice? What can only the Savior do?
“Thou, Lord, art good, and ready to forgive; and plenteous in mercy.”Psalm 86:5

Land it: Jesus is not merely able to forgive. Psalm 86 says He is ready.

04

Tell

8 minutes

A testimony does not have to explain everything. It can be one true sentence about what Jesus has done for you.

Two invitations

Psalm 66 gives two invitations. First: “Come and see” what God has done in history. Then: “Come and hear” what He has done in one person’s soul. A testimony uses both: the shared stories of scripture and the personal evidence of a life.

The psalmist does not claim to know everything about God. He tells the part he knows. That makes testimony possible even for a child.

“Come and see the works of God: he is terrible in his doing toward the children of men.”Psalm 66:5
“Come and hear … and I will declare what he hath done for my soul.”Psalm 66:16
“My mouth shall shew forth thy righteousness and thy salvation all the day.”Psalm 71:15

Psalm 71 imagines testimony continuing across an entire life: “O God, thou hast taught me from my youth,” and even when the writer is old, he wants to tell the next generation about God’s strength. The chain works only if somebody speaks.

One sentence each

Pick a doorway

Passing is always allowed. Teacher goes first with something brief and true.

Tap a name.
Before they answer
  • A testimony is not a performance.
  • Specific beats impressive.
  • “I prayed and felt calmer” is a complete testimony.

Land it: You do not need every answer to tell one true thing Jesus Christ has done for you.

05

Take it home

4 minutes
Psalm 86 is a prayer

David begins with “Bow down thine ear, O Lord, hear me.” He names the trouble, asks directly for help, remembers that God is “ready to forgive,” and then asks God to teach him how to walk. Prayer is not only asking God to change the situation. It is also asking Him to change the person walking through it.

Teacher“In the day of my trouble…”
Class“I will call upon thee.”
“For thou wilt answer me.”Psalm 86:7
“Teach me thy way, O Lord; I will walk in thy truth.”Psalm 86:11

Try the call-and-response quietly, then like they believe it, then once from memory.

This week

One memory. One person.

Choose one thing God did—in scripture or in your life—and tell one person. Ten seconds is enough.

Final sentence: Remember what He has done. Return when you wander. Tell somebody what you know.
