02

Meet Ezekiel.

4 minutes

Before we explore his pictures, meet the man who saw them: a priest far from home, called to help people who had lost so much.

JerusalemThe city and temple in Judah.
BabyloniaHe lived here among people taken into exile.
By the River ChebarThe Lord called him to speak to His people.
Tell the story · about 90 seconds

1. Taken far from home. Ezekiel was an Old Testament priest. Around 600 years before Jesus was born, the Babylonians took him and many other people from Judah into captivity. He lived among the exiles near the River Chebar in Babylonia. Exile means being forced to live away from your homeland.

2. A surprising call. There, far from Jerusalem, Ezekiel saw a remarkable vision: living creatures, wheels, and a throne. He described the glory of the Lord, fell facedown, and heard a voice. The Lord told him to stand; the Spirit helped him stand and listen. God sent him to speak His words to the people.

3. A difficult job. The Lord told Ezekiel that some people would refuse to listen, but he must still deliver the message. He was to be a watchman who warned them. Ezekiel lived among the exiles; he sat with them for seven days. He was part of this suffering community.

4. Warning—and hope. Ezekiel warned about Jerusalem’s fall. Later, a survivor brought the news that the city had been captured. Yet his book also looks forward: God would gather His people, change their hearts, and bring new life. That is why its pictures of a new heart, joined sticks, and a healing river matter.

Read Ezekiel 1:3 and 2:2–3. Ask: “Where was he? What did the Lord ask him to do?” Tell the story above in your own words; the class does not need to memorize every name or symbol.

Check the story

Far from home. Still called.

One memorable detail: the scroll that tasted like honey

In the account of his call, Ezekiel was given a scroll to eat, and it tasted sweet as honey. Then the Lord sent him to speak. We can connect this picture with receiving God’s words in our hearts before sharing them.

Read Ezekiel 3:1–4 and verse 10. This is a symbolic teaching picture, not an instruction for children to eat paper. Use this detail instead of one reveal question if the class is curious.

Step into the story

Imagine having to leave your home and the places you love. What could it mean to learn that God still knows where you are and has something for you to do?

God knew where Ezekiel was. His people were not beyond His reach.

A view from the tower.

5 minutes

A watchman stood high on a wall or tower, looking for danger. When trouble approached, the watchman warned the people so they could respond.

Scripture detectives

Hear. Speak. Help.

Read the whole verse. Point to your ears when you hear “hear” and your mouth when you hear “warning.” Then open the clues.

Stand up and practice · stay by your seat

The watchman’s trail.

Pretend to walk in place. Choose a volunteer to call “Low branch!” (duck), “Stream!” (pretend to step across), or “Stop!” (freeze). Then sit down.

Ask: “What did the warning help you do? How is a prophet’s message like that?”

Try a real-life connection

Ask for a teaching the children remember from the living prophet. Help them find a way to act on it. Listening to prophets helps us follow Jesus; it does not mean faithful people never face problems. Anyone may do the actions seated.

04

Two books. One Savior.

6 minutes

Read Ezekiel 37:15–17. Hold up one hand for each stick, then bring your hands together. No real sticks are needed.

The Lord told Ezekiel to join sticks marked for Judah and Joseph. The sign taught about bringing His divided people together. This week’s outline also connects the sticks with the Bible and the Book of Mormon: two witnesses that help us come to Jesus Christ.

Bring the witnesses together

What do both books teach?

Read the two passages aloud. Tap each book, then join their witnesses.

05

A heart that can change.

4 minutes
“A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you.”Read Ezekiel 36:26–27

Ezekiel’s “stony heart” is a picture of refusing to listen to God. A softer heart is willing to listen, love, and change. Jesus Christ can help us want what is good and act on it.

Turn toward Jesus

What could change next?

Pretend to hold a stone with a closed fist. Open your hand as you think of a choice that makes room for the Lord’s help.

The heart image is about our desires and choices, not a physical heart. Feeling sad, anxious, or angry does not make someone bad. Invite general examples; no one needs to describe a private mistake.

Jesus helps me change. I can begin with one willing choice.

06

Where the river goes.

6 minutes

Ezekiel saw a vision of water flowing out from the house of the Lord. Farther along, the stream became a river. Life grew where its healing water went.

Read Ezekiel 47:1, 3–5, then verses 8–9 and 12. Ask the class to listen for the water’s beginning, its depth, and what grew.

Explore the vision

A little stream. A river of life.

Tap the stages as you read. Point to your ankles, knees, and waist from your seat.

The water begins at the house of the Lord. · Verse 1

Find the temple in the picture. Where do the blessings begin?

From the picture to our lives

What did the water change? How can Jesus bring hope or healing to a person or family? Why do you think the river started at the house of the Lord?

We can connect this vision with the Savior’s blessings in the temple. As we make and keep covenants with Him, He helps us grow closer to Him and offers healing that reaches into our hearts and families.

One step now

You can turn toward Him today.

Sophie wants to prepare for the temple. Braxton wants to help his family feel more peaceful. What is one thing each could do now?

Try: learn about Jesus, pray, keep a promise, ask about a family story, or choose a kind word.

The changing diagram follows the scripture story; it does not measure anyone’s faith. Temple blessings do not promise every illness or family problem will disappear now. Children in every family situation belong and are loved. Anyone may answer about an imagined family.

Jesus Christ is the source of healing and new life.

Teacher notes, sources & timing

The seven sections total 30 minutes: 2 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 4 + 6 + 3. Prayers and transitions are included. Use the four story paragraphs as a brief telling script. Choose one or two responses per question; the suggested activities fill the lesson without the optional video. If time is short, use one biography reveal, match one scripture pair, and open one heart scenario. Treat the scroll detail as an alternative, not extra time. Keep the closing invitation.

No supplies are required beyond the screen. Scripture links open in another tab; a printed Bible and Book of Mormon are optional. Children can respond together, from their seats, with a partner, or silently. The named scenarios are imagined examples, not accounts of events in the children’s lives.

This lesson follows selected themes from the week’s reading rather than covering every chapter. The two-stick sign concerns the gathering and unity of Israel; the official outline also applies it to the Bible and Book of Mormon. The river picture is symbolic, not a modern scientific claim about the Dead Sea.

The hero is a newly generated artistic interpretation. Four imaginative class illustrations use the children’s reference photos for likeness: a watchtower lookout, two scripture paths joining, a heart-shaped garden gate, and a walk through the river vision. They are teaching metaphors, not claims about real events or literal scripture scenes. All seven children appear. All pictures enlarge; sound starts off. Printing expands the teaching answers and source notes.
