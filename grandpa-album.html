Why I went hunting for the CD

The MP3s of Grandpa's How Great Thou Art and Other Favorite Songs have been sitting in Dad's Google Drive for years. They were the obvious shortcut: just upload them to Spotify and call it done. I didn't, and this page is the receipt.

MP3s are pinned to 128 kbps because they were encoded at constant bitrate. 04Clipping

Digital audio has a ceiling: peak amplitude can't exceed ±1.0 (full scale). Push past that during encoding and the waveform gets flat-topped — audible distortion that's permanently baked into the file. The CD masters were properly mastered with headroom; the MP3 conversion pipeline pushed many tracks past the ceiling.

Peak amplitude per track
Each pair of bars is one track. The red dashed line at 1.0 is the digital clipping threshold. Bars that hit or cross it have distortion baked in.
FLAC peak MP3 peak
7 of 26 MP3 tracks have peaks at or above 1.0 — actual clipping events. 0 of 26 FLAC tracks reach the ceiling. The CD masters are clean; the MP3s were level-boosted past full scale at some point years ago, and that distortion is now permanent in those files.

05What MP3 throws away

MP3 is a lossy codec: it discards information that psychoacoustic models predict you can't hear. At 128 kbps it throws away a lot — especially the high-frequency "air" that gives vocal recordings their natural presence. Below is the percentage of each frequency band's energy that survives the MP3 conversion (volume-corrected, track 1 as sample).

Frequency content preserved by MP3
100% = MP3 keeps everything the FLAC has. Lower bars mean the MP3 discarded content in that band.

The cliff above 12 kHz is the classic 128 kbps MP3 signature. The band from 12 to 16 kHz — where vocal sibilance and "breath" sit — is two-thirds gone. Everything above 16 kHz is just deleted (less than 1% survives). On a phone speaker or laptop, you might not notice. On real headphones, you do.

06Null test

The most direct comparison: line up the FLAC and the MP3 sample for sample, match their volumes, subtract one from the other, and measure how much signal is left. A perfect re-encode leaves nothing.

A value of 0.1107 is roughly 100× louder than the "transparent" threshold and well past the casual-listening audibility threshold (~0.05). What's in that residual? The deleted high frequencies, MP3 pre-echo artifacts at consonants, and the distortion from the clipped peaks.

Why this mattered

  • Distortion would have been permanent. Uploading the MP3s would have sent 7 tracks with baked-in clipping to Spotify, Apple, and every other streaming service forever. Future generations of the family listening would hear those artifacts.
  • Streaming services re-encode anyway. Spotify converts uploads to 320 kbps Vorbis, Apple to 256 kbps AAC. Giving them FLAC means their encoders work from the cleanest possible source. Giving them 128 kbps MP3 means they re-compress an already-compressed file, layering artifacts on artifacts.
  • The fidelity gap is real. Grandpa and Elna's voices have nuance — sibilance, breath, the "air" of the room they recorded in — that lives in the 8-16 kHz band that MP3 mostly threw away. The FLAC version keeps it.
  • This is the canonical master from here on. If anyone in the family ever wants to remaster, sample, or reissue this album in the future, they'll work from the FLAC files in the project directory, not from a degraded MP3 copy.
