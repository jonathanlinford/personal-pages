Taking Grandma Nancy
to where her father went

In 1939, James Glenn Dyer sailed south with Admiral Byrd and wintered on a small island in Marguerite Bay. Eighty-some years later, his daughter is 85. This page is how we get her there, and the gentler ways to simply stand on Antarctica together.

The man we're chasing

James Glenn Dyer (1908–1995)

Surveyor, cartographer, and navigator. He didn't just visit Antarctica, he helped map a piece of it that still carries his name. Tap any moment to expand it.

★
1908 · Alamosa, Colorado
Born May 10 into a big farm family
One of eleven children of James Wesley Dyer and Beulah LaVert Bailey. He reportedly planted trees in Tennessee to earn money for school.
1933 · Georgia Tech
Degree in aeronautical engineering
Graduated into the Depression. Later surveyed for the U.S. government in Nevada, where he devised a faster sighting method using distant landmarks.
1939–41 · Antarctica
Navigator & cartographer, U.S. Antarctic Service Expedition
Answered Admiral Byrd's call. Wintered at East Base on Stonington Island in Marguerite Bay, and led the southeast sledging party. Survived a fall into a crevasse and a three-day storm on the high plateau.
⛰
Forever · Palmer Land
The Dyer Plateau is named for him
The broad ice upland he sledged across (70°30′S, 65°0′W) was named in his honor, the Dyer Plateau. It is interior Antarctica, reachable only by fly-in expedition, so it isn't part of any cruise. The bay he wintered in, however, is. (gazetteer entry)
1942–45 · WWII
Major, commanded a secret Arctic air base
Recruited by the Army Air Corps for his polar expertise. Commanded "Crystal III," a refueling base for bombers bound for Europe.
1956–74 · Weather Bureau
Returned south, then led Overseas Operations
Back to Antarctica in 1956–57 as a U.S. observer during the International Geophysical Year. Retired in 1974 as Director of Overseas Operations. Awarded the Antarctic Service Medal.
🤍
1995 · Utah
Died March 13 at his daughter Nancy's home
Survived by daughter Nancy and sons Robert, John, and Winston, 31 grandchildren, and 16 great-grandchildren. Buried in Salt Lake City.
His island
Stonington Island, in Marguerite Bay, below the Antarctic Circle.
His base
East Base, a U.S. National Historic Site (HSM 55), commissioned by FDR in 1939.
The bay's name
Charcot named Marguerite Bay in 1909 for his wife. Decades later, Dyer wintered in it.
Grandma Nancy
His daughter, born Dec 6, 1940. She is 85 now.
The decision

Two ways south

There is one hard trade-off. The exact place he stood is the longest, roughest, priciest voyage, and even then a landing is never guaranteed. The gentlest trips can't reach it at all. Pick a path to light its route on the map and see its options.

His voyage · into Marguerite Bay Easy fly-cruise · stops on the peninsula
Only one operator markets this. Oceanwide Expeditions runs the deep-south voyages that actually aim for Marguerite Bay, and they have a dedicated page about landing at East Base, calling the shore landing "easy" and "among the most popular research stations we visit."
How often do they actually make it? Straight from their Polar Travel Advisor (June 2026): they have "successfully reached Marguerite Bay and landed at Stonington Island on many of our recent deep-south voyages," but access is never guaranteed on any particular departure. Some seasons sea ice has restricted or blocked the bay; others have been very favorable. So: a real and recently good shot, not a promise.

The requirements

What the trip requires of every passenger

Straight from Oceanwide's Polar Travel Advisor (June 2026), these are the operator's stated requirements for the deep-south voyages on m/v Ortelius. They apply to everyone, regardless of age. This is a checklist to weigh against, not a verdict on anyone.

Oceanwide's words: their voyages "require passengers to meet a certain level of physical fitness and mobility," and the conditions "can be demanding even for those in excellent physical condition."

🚶 Mobility & physical

  • Move about the ship independently, including up and down stairs. The Ortelius has no elevator.
  • Navigate the gangway, often in adverse weather and sea conditions.
  • Embark and disembark Zodiacs (small inflatable boats) for every shore landing and transfer.
  • Take long walks ashore over uneven, snowy, and rocky terrain.
  • Tolerate rough seas, cold, and challenging polar weather.

🩺 Medical & paperwork

  • Medical clearance is required for every passenger before travel.
  • A qualified physician sails on every voyage, but the onboard infirmary provides first-aid and basic medical care only (no hospital).
  • Comprehensive travel insurance is mandatory, and must cover medical repatriation and emergency evacuation by helicopter, aircraft, or ship.
  • Insurance policy details are collected after booking.

🧭 Endurance & getting there

  • Long-haul flights to Ushuaia (via Buenos Aires), then 13 to 25 days aboard.
  • On these deep-south sailings, roughly 2 days each way across the open Drake Passage, plus open Bellingshausen Sea on the 25-day OTL27-27.
  • Remote, polar setting: outside help can be days away.

💵 What the mandatory insurance costs

  • Live-quoted June 3, 2026 (Squaremouth: age 85, $25,000 trip, Jan 12 to Feb 9 2027): 18 policies, $2,898 to $10,741 total for the trip.
  • Standout value: Travel Insured FlexiPAX, $3,603 ($100k primary medical, $500k medical evacuation, cancel-for-any-reason available).
  • Heaviest medical: IMG Travel LX Basic $7,592 or Travelex Ultimate $7,689 ($250k–500k medical, $1M evacuation).
  • Pre-existing conditions are covered only if the policy is bought within ~2–3 weeks of the first trip deposit.
  • At 85 some products age out (Medjet stops at 84); Ripcord/Redpoint covers to age 90 with a polar upgrade.
  • See the live quotes (no personal info attached) ↗
The fly-cruise paths are physically easier on the "getting there" front, they replace the open-ocean Drake crossing with a short charter flight, but the same landing requirements still apply: Zodiac embarking/disembarking, walking ashore on uneven ground, medical clearance, and mandatory evacuation insurance. Some larger fly-cruise ships do have elevators; the Ortelius does not.
